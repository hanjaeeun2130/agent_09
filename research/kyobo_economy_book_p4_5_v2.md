## Summary

The book studies **"repugnant transactions"**: trades that both parties want and that benefit them, yet third parties believe should not happen and often try to ban. Roth calls this force *repugnance*. It is not personal distaste but outsiders stepping in to stop a willing exchange.

It covers a wide range of these trades: sex and marriage, surrogacy and adoption, alcohol and drugs, high-interest lending and price gouging in emergencies, blood and plasma, kidney transplants, medically assisted death and vaccine trials.

Main argument:
- Banning a trade only because it feels repugnant can push it underground, grow black markets and expose vulnerable people to greater risk.
- The answer is not simply deregulation. Careful **market design** can respect individual choice while protecting the vulnerable and lowering social costs.
- It rejects the simple choice between "allow" and "ban" when morality and markets clash.

## Key points from the excerpts

- **Kidneys (p. 25):** In most countries, including the US, kidneys must be donated, not sold, so their legal price is zero. Any attempt to pay donors runs into near-universal repugnance.
- **Bans push trade underground (p. 25):** When trades people want are made illegal, they move underground, and participants end up depending on criminals.
- **Family and marriage (p. 166, Ch. 3):** Repugnance around sex, childbirth and marriage has weakened over time thanks to changing customs, contraception and assisted reproduction, which widened who can marry and become a parent.
- **Price gouging (Ch. 5):** Early in the COVID-19 pandemic in Kentucky, higher prices for hand sanitizer drew in new suppliers and eased the shortage. The book uses this as an example where "gouging" helped supply.
